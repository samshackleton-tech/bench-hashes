Devon: the boundary you propose (a straight-line wide kernel returning raw sums to a word-level loop) is the one I would choose, and half of what it needs exists as of tonight (`de232955` on my branch). The design, in the order I would build it:

**1. The word-level caller, now: the kernel behind a contract the caller assumes.**
`callee NAME at ADDRESS assumes CONTRACT (N) budget BYTES standin FN` in the source. The caller's theorem is then about its own instructions over any program in which that address meets the contract (`NAME_meets (ext) (hA : ∀ ..., Abi.Meets (Code_NAME.prog ext) ADDRESS (CONTRACT ...))`): the assumption is a hypothesis in the reader's sight, not a line of the model. When examples run, a function of ours (`standin`) stands at the kernel's address: the same contract, slowly, in instructions the model has. I used it for BLAKE3's 16-lane parent kernel (`Spec_p16.lean`, `p16s`, `tree32` in `levels/levels.ode`): `tree32` is proved so, and its examples run over the stand-in. For Adler-32 that gives you the caller's proof and the append law's use today, with a scalar stand-in for the 4 KiB kernel. A kernel's result in RAX is already a contract's `returns`.

**2. The kernel's own proof: `prove.py` with a `returns` clause, on a wide model.**
`contract NAME { spec; reads; writes }` gets `returns rax: EXPR`; the generator's last goal is then about RAX at the `ret`. That part is small and mine. The model is the cost: I would not widen `one/Machine.lean`'s sixteen 128-bit registers first (every proof on the branch is regenerated when the state's shape changes). I would grow `wide/Wide.lean` (32 registers of 512 bits, three instructions, no memory today) into the kernels' model: your retained forms, loads from memory, the XMM and YMM views with the VEX and EVEX zeroing rule, and a `Meets` of its own. Each form with its row against a processor before it counts. Your list of retained forms is the right size for that: `vpdpbusd` is the one with semantics worth a careful sentence.

**3. One model.** When the wide model has what `one/Machine.lean` has, the kernel's theorem is an `Abi.Meets` and discharges the caller's hypothesis: no assumption left. Until then the theorem says what it rests on, and the stand-in shows the contract can be met.

**Read-only data: say it as memory the kernel reads, and check the image like the text.**
Folding a constant into the instruction (`.c`, as `lift_one.py` does for the 16-byte loads) puts the data's reading in the lifter's trust, as you say. For 64-byte tables I would not extend that. The contract gets a piece: `(Bytes.layout 64).keeps WEIGHTS bytes` at the table's address in the image (our programs sit at fixed addresses in the model, so a RIP-relative load is a load at a known address), and the theorem is then about "these instructions, with these bytes at that address". What binds the image is one check for text and data alike: the linked object's bytes at each symbol are the bytes the theorem names (a script today, as the lifter is; Lean reading the object's bytes when the decoder exists, which covers both in one statement). Or no table at all: constants made by immediates, as the AArch64 hex kernel now makes its sixteen characters (two `movz`/`movk` runs and two moves into a vector register); for byte-ones and the weights 1..64 that may cost less than the binding.

If the measurement says the kernel is worth it, send the assembled forms and I start on 2; 1 you can use as it is.
